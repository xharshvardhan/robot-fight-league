Fighter Brain

What the networks in the fight league are, how they are wired and how they were made. State as of 13 Nov 2026.

Short answer. Each fighter is run by a small, plain neural network: numbers go in one side, pass through three layers, and joint commands come out the other. It is the simplest kind there is (a "multilayer perceptron").

It is not a two-tower model. There is a second network during training, the "critic", which is where the two-network impression comes from. It only helps training and is thrown away for the fight.

Size: about 233,000 adjustable numbers. A phone photo filter is bigger. It runs 50 times a second.

1. What a neural network is here

A function with a lot of dials. It takes a list of numbers describing the robot's situation and returns a list of numbers saying where each joint should move next. Training turns the dials until the outputs earn good scores. Nothing is written by hand about how to punch or balance.

2. The fighter network (red corner)

The amber, red and blue inputs are what make it a fighter. The rest is the same body sense every walking robot network uses.

No memory. It decides from the present instant only, plus its own last command. It does not remember what the opponent did a second ago.
No eyes. It never sees an image. The opponent exists only as "target point" and "nearest incoming fist", both worked out for it by the simulator.
No plan. It does not choose when to strike or defend. A hand-written rule sets the skill switch (next section).

3. Red corner and blue corner are built differently

Red is the fighter we are developing. Blue is a fixed sparring partner made from the specialists and does not learn.

4. Where the "second network" comes from: actor and critic

During training every fighter has two networks side by side. This is the standard recipe (called PPO), not something special to this project.

The two do not share any layers and do not talk to each other in a fight, which is why this is not a two-tower model.

7. What this design can't do yet

  • Choose its own tactics. The rule decides strike, defend or get up. A fighter that picks for itself needs the switch removed or a small "coach" network on top.
  • Aim by itself. The strike target is handed to it (a point between the opponent's belly and head). It does not decide where to hit.
  • React to patterns. With no memory it cannot notice that the opponent always leads with the left.
  • Walk during a fight. The walking skill is in the network but the rule never selects it; footwork currently comes from the strike skill stepping toward its target.
